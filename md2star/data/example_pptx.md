# The 10/20/30 pitch deck

## After Guy Kawasaki

<!--
Attribution: Guy Kawasaki, "The 10/20/30 Rule of PowerPoint"
(blog post, 2005) and "The Only 10 Slides You Need in Your
Pitch" (2015). Ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty-point

-->

***

# The rule {.big}

**10 slides · 20 minutes · 30-point minimum font**

<!--
"I'm evangelizing the 10/20/30 Rule of PowerPoint. It's quite
simple: a PowerPoint presentation should have ten slides, last
no more than twenty minutes, and contain no font smaller than
thirty points." — Kawasaki, 2005.
-->

***

# Why ten slides

Ten is the optimal number of slides in a pitch — because a
normal human being cannot comprehend more than ten concepts in
a meeting.

*And venture capitalists are especially incapable of remembering
more than ten concepts.*

<!-- Kawasaki, "The 10/20/30 Rule of PowerPoint", 2005. -->

***

# Why twenty minutes

You have an hour on the calendar.

Sure — but you are also using a Windows laptop, so it will take
forty minutes to make it work with the projector.

That leaves you twenty. Plan for twenty.

<!--
The Windows-projector joke is Kawasaki's original 2005 line.
It has aged; the point still holds: setup steals the room.
-->

***

# Why thirty-point font {.big}

Rule of thumb: take the age of the oldest person in the audience
and divide by two. That's your font size.

<!--
"Take the age of the oldest person in the audience and divide
it by two. That's your optimum font size." — Kawasaki, 2005.
The corollary: if you catch yourself dropping to 20 pt to fit
more text, you don't know your material well enough. **Total
bozosity.**
-->

***

# 1 · Title

Company name, one-line pitch, contact, date.

<!-- Who you are. What you do. How to reach you. -->

***

# 2 · Problem or opportunity

The pain the customer feels today. Concrete, not jargon.

<!-- A specific customer's story beats an abstract description. -->

***

# 3 · Value proposition

What you sell, and why the customer buys it.

<!-- The outcome, not the feature list. -->

***

# 4 · Underlying magic

The technology, secret sauce, or unfair advantage.

<!-- Diagram beats paragraph. Show, don't tell. -->

***

# 5 · Business model

Who pays you, how much, how often.

<!-- One line, no pricing matrix. -->

***

# 6 · Go-to-market plan

How you reach the first thousand customers *without* buying ads.

<!-- Name a distribution channel, not a marketing budget. -->

***

# 7 · Competitive analysis

Who the customer tries today — and why they'll switch.

<!-- Include "do nothing" as a competitor. It usually wins. -->

***

# 8 · Team

The people, their scars, and what's missing.

<!-- Name the gap; that gap is the ask. -->

***

# 9 · Financials and key metrics

Three years, three lines: revenue, gross margin, burn.

<!-- Round numbers beat false precision. -->

***

# 10 · Status and use of funds

Where you are, what's next, how much you need, and for what.

<!-- Milestones the money buys, not just months of runway. -->

***

# In summary {.big}

Ten slides. Twenty minutes. Thirty-point font.

Drop below 30 pt to cram more text, and — in Kawasaki's own word
— that is **total bozosity**.
